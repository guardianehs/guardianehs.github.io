Why Bengaluru's garbage crisis keeps returning

Arun Williams · 6 min read

The smell is the honest part. Residents describe waking in the middle of the night because of it, closing doors that don't help, walking market streets with a hand over the nose. Everyone blames the trucks. The trucks are rarely the problem.

Bengaluru's slide from garden city to garbage city didn't happen in one bad season. It happened one skipped processing site at a time.

A collection failure it isn't

The story repeats on a schedule: heaps appear on roadsides, a court or an ombudsman opens an inquiry, officials are summoned, a report is promised, the heaps clear for a few weeks, and the cycle resets. In April 2026, a Lokayukta inspection of Gottigere, Anjanapura and JP Nagar found exactly what residents had already been reporting — garbage stacked along roads in a city generating 5,000–6,000 tonnes of waste a day. The instinct is to read this as a failure to collect. It isn't. Trucks are running. What they're short of is somewhere legal to put what they pick up.

A ward can segregate and collect waste every single day and still fail the city, if nowhere inside that ward is licensed to process what it collects.

Roughly 55–60% of Bengaluru's daily waste is organic. Under the Solid Waste Management Rules that took effect in April 2026, that fraction isn't supposed to travel far — it's meant to be processed close to where it's generated. What's actually happened, repeatedly, is that ward-level collection tenders get issued before any approved processing site exists for that ward. Wet waste gets picked up on schedule and then has nowhere to go, so it gets stockpiled — at quarries pushed past capacity, in stormwater drains, on roadsides, or trucked toward villages that have started physically blocking the trucks.

What a real audit would need to check

  • Site identification first. No ward-level tender should go out before a processing site for that ward is confirmed and registered — not proposed, not "under discussion."
  • Capacity, not just existence. A registered site handling a fraction of a ward's daily tonnage is a paper fix, not a working one.
  • Chain of custody. Waste needs to be traceable from collection point to processing site, so "stockpiled pending disposal" stops being an acceptable answer at any stage.
  • Independent verification. Self-reported compliance from the same body being investigated is why this cycle has survived a decade of inquiries.

Accountability has to sit with whoever holds the premises

Ward-level fixes only cover the public-collection side of this. A large share of what ends up on the roadside starts on private premises — apartment complexes, commercial buildings, rented layouts — whose owners treat "put it out for the truck" as the end of their responsibility. It isn't. A landlord who leases out a premises is generating waste on that land as surely as any tenant, and the obligation to segregate it and see it through to a licensed processor doesn't transfer away just because someone else is occupying the space.

That accountability needs to be explicit, not assumed: segregation at source enforced as a condition of occupancy, proof of safe disposal through a verified handler kept on file, and a named responsible person per premises — not a diffuse "building management" nobody can pin down when the pile shows up outside the gate. Municipal bodies can register every ward's processing site and it still won't matter if the waste arriving at the curb was never segregated properly in the building it came from.

The response has to be blunt

A hearing that ends in a promised report is not enforcement — it's a scheduling exercise, and Bengaluru has been through this exact sequence before, under different governments, over different landfill villages, back to at least 2012. The Lokayukta summoning BSWML's leadership is a real lever only if it produces a binding condition — no tender without a registered site — not another status update due in ninety days.

What none of this fixes

An approved site register doesn't generate political will to fund it, doesn't survive a monsoon that floods half the identified plots, and doesn't change a household's habit of putting wet and dry waste in the same bag. None of the current proposals — CCTV at dumping hotspots, GPS-tracked trucks, a proposed garbage cess — solve the sequencing problem on their own. They make non-compliance easier to see. They don't make compliance easier to do.

That's the honest limit here. The fix isn't cleverness, it's sequencing: identify the site, register it, then tender the ward — in that order, every time, with no exceptions carved out under pressure to show fast results. Everything else is cosmetic until that sequence holds.
